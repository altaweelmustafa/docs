## The core PRNG requirement

For a cryptographic application, the basic requirement is that **an adversary who doesn't know the seed cannot determine the pseudorandom output**. If a PRNG's bit stream feeds a stream cipher, knowledge of that stream lets an adversary recover plaintext directly. Even for a PRF generating a key (say, a 128-bit key normally safe from brute force), a PRF that doesn't produce effectively random output could let an adversary narrow the search space and make brute force practical again.

This general secrecy requirement breaks down into three areas: **randomness**, **unpredictability**, and **seed characteristics**.

---

## Randomness testing: SP 800-22

**SP 800-22** lists 15 separate statistical tests of randomness. Three representative examples:

- **Frequency test** — the most basic test, required in any test suite. Checks whether the number of ones and zeros is approximately what a truly random sequence would produce.
- **Runs test** — examines the total number of *runs* (uninterrupted sequences of identical bits bounded by the opposite value on each side), checking whether run lengths match what's expected for random data.
- **Maurer's universal statistical test** — looks at the number of bits between matching patterns (related to how compressible the sequence is). A sequence that can be significantly compressed without loss is considered non-random.

---

## Seed requirements and generation

The seed feeding a PRNG must itself be **secure and unpredictable** — since the PRNG is deterministic, an adversary who deduces the seed can reproduce the entire output. In fact, **the seed itself must be a random or pseudorandom number**, typically generated by a TRNG (the scheme SP800-90 recommends).

This raises an obvious question: if a TRNG is available, why not just use it directly? Two practical reasons:

1. **Bandwidth/logistics** — for a stream cipher, a TRNG would require generating a keystream as long as the entire plaintext and transmitting it securely to the receiver. A PRNG needs only a short key (128–256 bits) delivered securely; the PRNG regenerates the rest.
2. **Bias correction** — even for a PRF needing only a limited number of bits, it's often better to seed a PRNG/PRF from a TRNG rather than use the TRNG's output directly, because a TRNG may produce a biased binary string; running it through a PRF conditions the output and removes that bias.
3. TRNGs may also simply be too slow to keep up with an application's demand for random bits.

---

## What to remember for the exam

A PRNG is cryptographically sound only if an adversary who doesn't know the seed can't predict the output — this requirement decomposes into randomness, unpredictability, and seed security. SP 800-22 provides 15 statistical randomness tests (frequency, runs, and Maurer's universal test are the representative examples to know). The seed itself must be random/unpredictable and is typically produced by a TRNG — used to seed a PRNG rather than directly, for bandwidth and bias-correction reasons.
