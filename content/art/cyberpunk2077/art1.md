In Cyberpunk 2077, Saburo Arasaka does something that has fascinated humans since the beginning of civilization: he cheats death. **They store his consciousness, copy it, and load into another body**, his _son's_ body.
It is fiction, but it draws from a real and accelerating field: what if we could one day save a person's mind and put it somewhere else? The question sounds like science fiction, but serious researchers are working on exactly that.
And if it ever becomes possible, it would force **every major religion (Islam included)** to answer some uncomfortable questions.

## What Would It Actually Take?

The leading scientific topic about it is **whole-brain emulation (WBE)**: mapping every neuron and connection with enough fidelity to simulate the brain's behavior in software (or another criteria).

We have fully mapped the connectome of *C. elegans*, a worm with 302 neurons.
The human brain has roughly 86 billion. 
Projects like Blue Brain are doing partial cortical column simulations, but full WBE is likely decades away at minimum, and may require physics we don't yet have **(like quantum physics, for instance)**.

Even if the technology comes tomorrow, it would immediately run into a wall that no amount of computing power can break the **hard problem of consciousness**. Even a perfect copy of all neurons raises an unanswerable question:
is the copy **you**, or just a very convincing **duplicate**? The original still died. This is not a technical limitation waiting to be engineered away.
It is a philosophical deadlock about what continuity of identity actually means, and it has no clean resolution. Arasaka's Soulkiller in Cyberpunk is honest about this, Silverhand's engram even acknowledges he might just be an echo, not the real person.

## What Islam Says?

This is where the conversation becomes theologically serious, and Islamic scholars would likely converge on a clear position.

**The ruh is not data.** In Islam, the soul *(ruh)* is not reducible to brain states or patterns of information. Allah says in the Quran:

> _"They ask you about the ruh. Say: the ruh is from the command of my Lord, and you have been given of knowledge only a little."_ — Al-Isra 17:85

The _ruh_ is a divine creation that Allah gives and takes.
No technology can replicate, copy, or transfer it. What neuroscience can map: synaptic weights, behavioral patterns, memories, personality, corresponds at best to the _nafs al-hayawaniyya_, the biological-animal layer of the self.
The consciousness, the thing that actually experiences being you, is entirely another story.

So what would actually be "uploaded"?
A very sophisticated **simulation of a person**: their habits, speech patterns, memories, and responses.
Not the person. The _ruh_ would already be gone,
taken at the moment of death as it always is.
What remains in the machine is an orphan: a human-shaped pattern with no soul, that **the world might mistakenly treat as someone it is not**.

**Resurrection belongs to Allah alone.**
Islamic theology is explicit that *Ba'th* **(resurrection)** is a divine act performed by Allah on Yawm al-Qiyama.
He reconstructs the person, body and soul, in a way beyond current human realization.
A digital copy does not get involved with this, because it is not the person.
The original still dies, still faces death, still has their *ruh* taken by *Malak el-Maut*.
The upload changes nothing about their akhira.

As for the ruling on the technology itself, scholars would likely distinguish between the act and its framing. Creating such a copy is probably permissible in the same way that recording someone's voice or image is permissible. Treating the copy as if it were the actual living person, however, would be a form of deception. And claiming the technology constitutes resurrection or immortality would be far more serious — it would border on usurping one of Allah's exclusive attributes, _Al-Muhyi_, the Giver of Life.

## Where That Leaves Us?

The technology is not here yet, but the assumption is real.
Some form of mind-copying will probably exist within this century. 
When it arrives, it will be one of the most disorienting developments in human history, not because it solves the problem of death; but because it will convincingly appear to.

For Muslims, the theological picture remains stable. The *ruh* cannot be uploaded.
Death remains death.
Resurrection remains Allah's domain, not a feature to be replicated in a data center.
The copy that emerges would be something genuinely new, not the deceased, not a monster, but a human-shaped echo carrying someone else's memories, navigating a world that doesn't quite know what it is.

Saburo Arasaka essentially learned this. What got back wasn't really him, it was a **new incarnation of him**.
