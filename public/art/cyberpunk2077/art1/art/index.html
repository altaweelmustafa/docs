Immortality and Resurrection: Possibility and How It Affects Religion

posted on 2026 Jul 09

In Cyberpunk 2077, Saburo Arasaka does something that has fascinated humans since the beginning of civilization — he cheats death. His consciousness is preserved, copied, and loaded into another body. It is fiction, but it draws from a real and accelerating field of inquiry: what if we could one day save a person’s mind and put it somewhere else? The question sounds like science fiction, but serious researchers are working on exactly that. And if it ever becomes possible, it would force every major religion — Islam included — to answer some uncomfortable questions.

What Would It Actually Take?

The leading scientific candidate is whole-brain emulation (WBE) — mapping every neuron, synapse, and connection with enough fidelity to simulate the brain’s behavior in software or another substrate. We have fully mapped the connectome of C. elegans, a worm with 302 neurons. The human brain has roughly 86 billion. Projects like Blue Brain are doing partial cortical column simulations, but full WBE is likely decades away at minimum, and may require physics we don’t yet have — the role of quantum effects in microtubules, for instance, is still debated.

Even if the technology arrived tomorrow, it would immediately run into a wall that no amount of computing power can break: the hard problem of consciousness. Even a perfect copy of every neuron raises an unanswerable question — is the copy you, or just a very convincing duplicate? The original still died. This is not a technical limitation waiting to be engineered away. It is a philosophical deadlock about what continuity of identity actually means, and it has no clean resolution. Arasaka’s Soulkiller in Cyberpunk is honest about this — Silverhand’s engram even acknowledges he might just be an echo, not the real person.

What Islam Has to Say

This is where the conversation becomes theologically serious, and Islamic scholars would likely converge on a clear position.

The ruh is not data. In Islam, the soul (ruh) is not reducible to brain states or patterns of information. Allah says in the Quran:

“They ask you about the ruh. Say: the ruh is from the command of my Lord, and you have been given of knowledge only a little.” — Al-Isra 17:85

The ruh is a divine creation that Allah gives and takes. No technology can replicate, copy, or transfer it. What neuroscience can map — synaptic weights, behavioral patterns, memories, personality — corresponds at best to the nafs al-hayawaniyya, the biological-animal layer of the self. The conscious subject underneath, the thing that actually experiences being you, is another matter entirely.

So what would actually be “uploaded”? A very sophisticated simulation of a person — their habits, speech patterns, memories, and responses. Not the person. The ruh would already be gone, taken at the moment of death as it always is. What remains in the machine is an orphan: a human-shaped pattern with no soul, that the world might mistakenly treat as someone it is not.

Resurrection belongs to Allah alone. Islamic theology is explicit that Ba’th — resurrection — is a divine act performed by Allah on Yawm al-Qiyama. He reconstitutes the person, body and soul, in a manner beyond current human comprehension. A digital copy does not preempt this or compete with it, because it is not the person. The original still dies, still faces death, still has their ruh taken by Azrael. The upload changes nothing about their akhira.

As for the ruling on the technology itself, scholars would likely distinguish between the act and its framing. Creating such a copy is probably permissible in the same way that recording someone’s voice or image is permissible. Treating the copy as if it were the actual living person, however, would be a form of deception. And claiming the technology constitutes resurrection or immortality would be far more serious — it would border on usurping one of Allah’s exclusive attributes, Al-Muhyi, the Giver of Life.

Where That Leaves Us

The technology is not here yet, but the trajectory is real. Some form of mind-copying will probably exist within this century, even if crude. When it arrives, it will be one of the most disorienting developments in human history — not because it solves the problem of death, but because it will convincingly appear to.

For Muslims, the theological picture remains stable. The ruh cannot be uploaded. Death remains death. Resurrection remains Allah’s domain, not a feature to be replicated in a data center. The copy that emerges would be something genuinely new — not the deceased, not a monster, but a human-shaped echo carrying someone else’s memories, navigating a world that doesn’t quite know what it is.

Arasaka essentially learned this with Saburo. What he got back wasn’t really his son.
