Guilty but Why?

posted on 2026 Jul 09

by Sejaa Altaweel

Ever felt trapped in a meaningless maze not knowing what got you in it in the first place? Each step you think you are making forward takes you back to square one? How many times have you tried to do the “right thing” but then discover that the rules seem to change everyday?

Franz Kafka’s The Trial is not about crime, it is about a man that wakes up in the morning suddenly discovers that the world no longer makes sense. The unsettling part of it all, is that Kafka did not only illustrate society in the 20th century, but how familiar it feels.. even today.

Josef K. is waken up by two “policemen” that puts him “under arrest”, without any accusation, without interrogation, without reason and even without being taken to jail.

It’s true that you are under arrest, but that shouldn’t stop you from carrying out your job. And there shouldn’t be anything to stop you carrying on with your usual life.

Meaning they are normalazing the concept of being under arrest, it is nothing but usual and unsurprising.

Josef K. simply continues living his life, with the horror of not knowing. He exists inside an invisible system with a faceless authority, that wants something from him, though he cannot understand what nor why nor how he can defend himself as he has zero information about what he is guilty of.

There was no guilt. The trial was nothing but a big piece of business.

Josef K. starts the story confidant, even arrogant. He thinks he can charm his way out of all of this because he is pretty sure he is innocent. He thinks he can use reason and logic with the court. But the more he tries, the more he realizes that the system is not interested in explanations, not even justice. He discovers that the court operates in endless layers, random rooms filled with jobless judges helpless lawyers useless files, yet the decisions are already made before the trial even begins.

Kafka was not describing a specific government or a political system. He was describing modern life. He was describing the feeling of being trapped inside processes you cannot influence, immigration forms, financial institutions, social rules, expectations. He was describing the universal fear that no matter how good you are, no matter how hard you try, there is always a judgment waiting for you, that you cannot see.

You’ve not behaved towards us the way we deserve after being so good to you, you forget that we, whatever we are, we’re still free men and you are not.

Kafka insists on the fact that systems can trap us without needing to explain how, they can make us feel guilty even when we’ve done nothing wrong. It is the guilt society teaches us, the guilt of not being enough.

Josef K. was a normal guy. Not a hero, nor a villain. An ambitious chief clerk at a large bank who earned trust and respect of most of the people surrounding him. After dealing with his trial for a whole year his job performance suffers and all his relationships collapses, not because any action from the court, but because his fear of the court.. and his endless confusion over what he did wrong.

Kafka shows that oppressive systems do not need to punish you. They simply need you to fear punishment. And you will destroy yourself all by your own hands.

But why didn’t Josef K. knowing that he was not guilty just walk away? Why did he accept the system instead of rejecting it? Kafka reveals that The Trial is now a mirror, if you were accused of something mysterious and undefined, would you resist, or would a part of you wonder if they are right and you actually did something wrong without knowing? Kafka proves that we internalize blame and quickly doubt ourselves and surrender to systems that treat us guilty. So Josef K. obeys, not because he must, but because he thinks he should.

And then comes the ending, two men arrive and take Josef K. out the city to kill him. Josef K. follows in silence and full obedience.

The only thing I can do now is keep my common sense and do what’s needed right till the end. I always wanted to go at the world and try and do too much, and even to do it for something that was not too cheap. That was wrong of me.

The Trial ends with Josef K. whispering “like a dog”, he dies believing the system was right without getting any explanation. The Trial ends not with clarity not with justice, but with silence. Because it was never about Josef K. alone, it was about us. And it will remain powerful because it forces us to ask: Where in your own life do you feel like Josef K.? Where do you obey rules you don’t understand? Where do you feel judged without explanation?
